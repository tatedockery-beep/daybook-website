1. The Service

Daybook is business software for residential contractors: clients and properties, leads, estimates, job scheduling, a field portal for technicians, time tracking, invoicing, payments, inventory, and related tools. It has two sides — an office side for owners and staff, and a technician side for field workers.

2. Your account and your people

You're responsible for your account credentials, for the people you invite, and for what they do in your account. Keep logins private. Two-factor authentication is available and recommended for owners and office staff.

A seat is one person who can sign in to your account, counted once — office or field, owner or technician. Invitations that haven't been accepted don't count, and neither do people you've suspended or removed. Someone becomes a seat the moment their access goes live, and stops being one when you remove it.

You may not share one login among several people to avoid seats. Beyond the billing question, shared logins break the record of who did what, which is the point of the audit trail.

5. When your bill changes

This section exists because surprise upcharges are the most common complaint about software in this category, and we've built the opposite behavior into the product rather than promising it in prose.

Going up — never automatic

When you try to add a person who would push you past your band's seat limit, Daybook blocks the invitation and tells you the exact price of the next band. An owner then confirms the upgrade. Only that confirmation changes your subscription. Our own background reconciliation, which keeps Stripe in step with your account, is deliberately not permitted to raise a bill under any circumstance — if it finds a mismatch that would cost you more, it leaves it alone and waits for a human.

The practical consequence, stated plainly: you can hit a wall when hiring. We think a wall you can see is better than an invoice you can't.

Going down — automatic, at renewal

If your headcount falls into a cheaper band, the lower price takes effect from your next invoice, without you asking. There's no mid-cycle refund for the period already paid, and a downgrade never needs your confirmation, because it only ever lowers what you pay.

6. Cancelling, and not paying

You can cancel at any time from Settings. Cancelling stops future billing; it doesn't refund the current period, and you keep full access until that period ends.

If the trial ends without a subscription, or a payment fails, access degrades in steps rather than switching off:

  • Full access — during the trial, while subscribed, and for the first 7 days after a failed payment, so a declined card doesn't stop your Tuesday.
  • Read-only — after an unsubscribed trial ends, and from day 8 to day 30 of a failed payment. You can still see everything, finish work already in flight by editing existing jobs, estimates, and invoices, and export all your data. You can't create new records or change settings.
  • Locked — after 30 days unpaid, or if the subscription is cancelled or terminated at Stripe. Billing and account pages stay reachable so you can pay and restore access.

At no point in that ladder do we delete your business data for non-payment. Getting your data out is not gated behind being a paying customer.

9. Estimates and signatures

Daybook can capture a customer's signature on an estimate in person, or an approval by email link. Either way the record binds to the exact scope and total the customer saw: the line items, the amount, and the consent wording are stored as an immutable snapshot at the moment of signing.

Consequently, if you change the scope or the price of an approved estimate, the approval is automatically voided and the estimate returns to a revised state needing a fresh approval. Edits that don't touch scope or price — internal notes, for instance — leave the approval intact. This is deliberate and can't be switched off: an approval that survived a price change wouldn't be worth anything.

The agreement recorded is between you and your customer. Daybook stores the evidence; we don't guarantee its legal sufficiency in your jurisdiction, and you should confirm that the consent wording meets your needs.
